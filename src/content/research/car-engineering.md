A chimeric antigen receptor is a piece of synthetic biology: an antibody fragment
that recognises a tumour marker, fused to the signalling machinery that tells an
immune cell to kill. CAR-T cells built this way have transformed the treatment of
blood cancers. Solid tumours have been far more stubborn.

We work on the parts of the problem that CAR-T cells cannot solve on their own.

**Different cells, different jobs.** T cells are not the only option. Neutrophils
cross the blood–brain barrier. Macrophages eat what they kill and present the
debris to the rest of the immune system. Natural killer cells do not cause
graft-versus-host disease, so they can come from a stranger. We build CARs into
all of them.

**Modular targeting.** A CAR is normally welded to one antigen; if the tumour stops
displaying it, the therapy stops working. Adapter systems decouple the two — the
cell recognises a small universal tag, and a separately dosed adapter molecule
decides what carries that tag. One cell product, many targets, no re-engineering.

**Synthetic memory.** Engineered cells fade. We build circuits that let a cell
sense a hallmark of the tumour microenvironment and respond by arming itself
further, rather than exhausting.

## Why this matters

Every element here is aimed at the same gap: therapies that work in a dish and in
a mouse, but lose their potency in the disorganised, immunosuppressive interior
of a human solid tumour.
