A therapy that works in a dish has to arrive somewhere specific in a person, at
the right moment, in the right amount. Most of the time that is a materials
problem rather than a biology problem.

## Cells as carriers

An immune cell that can find a tumour is also a vehicle that can carry something
there. Glioblastoma is the clearest case: the blood–brain barrier keeps most
drugs out of the brain, but neutrophils cross it routinely as part of normal
inflammation. A CAR-neutrophil carrying a drug-loaded nanoparticle uses that
biology as a delivery route.

The materials are designed to be **conditionally active** — intact in
circulation, disassembling in response to a feature of the target site: its
acidity, its redox state, the enzymes it secretes. The therapeutic window comes
from the material, not from the dose.

## Delivering into joints

Cartilage is dense, avascular and hard to reach. We work on nanoparticle–
cartilage interactions and pathology-based intra-articular delivery, including
nanomaterials that address osteoarthritis pain directly rather than only its
structural progression.

## Materials that rebuild bone

Regenerating bone needs a scaffold that does more than hold a space.
Magnesium-based implants and nanocomposites, periosteum-inspired membranes, and
tissue-mimetic membranes for the tendon–bone interface all work by recruiting
and instructing the host's own cells — including the immune cells that decide
whether the repair proceeds.

## The other half of the question

Anything implanted or injected has to be safe, and nanomaterials have a
complicated record on this. We study nanotoxicity alongside efficacy — how
particles distribute, what they do to cells that were never the target, and what
is left behind.
