An immune cell that can find a tumour is also a vehicle that can carry something
there. This is the idea behind much of our materials work: attach a drug-loaded
nanoparticle to an engineered cell, let the cell do the navigating, and release
the payload only once it arrives.

Glioblastoma is the clearest case. The blood–brain barrier keeps most drugs out
of the brain, but neutrophils cross it routinely as part of normal inflammation.
A CAR-neutrophil carrying a nanodrug uses that biology as a delivery route.

The materials themselves are designed to be **conditionally active**. They stay
intact in circulation and disassemble in response to a feature of the tumour
microenvironment — its acidity, its redox state, the enzymes it secretes. The
therapeutic window comes from the material, not from the dose.

## The other half of the question

Anything injected into a person has to be safe, and nanomaterials have a
complicated record on this. We study nanotoxicity alongside efficacy — how
particles distribute, what they do to cells that were never the target, and what
is left behind.
